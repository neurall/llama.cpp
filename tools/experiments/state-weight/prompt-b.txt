# Sourdough bread at home: a slow weekend loaf

Baking sourdough is mostly waiting. A starter, flour, water and salt are all you need, but the time you give each step decides the loaf. Feed the starter twice the evening before, one part starter, one part water and one part flour by weight, and keep it somewhere around twenty-four degrees. By the next morning it should have doubled and smell sweet and slightly sour, like yogurt and green apples. If it still smells of acetone, it is hungry: feed it again and wait two more hours.

Mix five hundred grams of bread flour with three hundred and seventy grams of water and let the dough rest, covered, for thirty minutes. This rest, called the autolyse, lets the flour drink the water so the gluten forms with almost no kneading. Then add one hundred grams of active starter and ten grams of fine salt, and squeeze everything together with wet hands until it holds. Over the next three hours fold the dough every thirty minutes: lift one side, stretch it up and fold it over the middle, turn the bowl a quarter turn, and repeat four times. The dough becomes smoother, springier and a little airy each round.

When the dough has grown by about half and shows bubbles along the sides of the bowl, tip it onto a lightly floured table, shape it into a tight round by dragging it toward you across the surface, and let it rest for twenty minutes. Shape it a second time into a batard or a boule, place it seam side up in a floured basket, cover it, and slide it into the refrigerator for twelve to sixteen hours. The cold slows the yeast but not the bacteria, which is where the flavor comes from.

In the morning heat the oven to two hundred and forty degrees with a heavy cast iron pot inside for forty-five minutes. Turn the cold dough onto a sheet of parchment, score the top with a razor blade in one confident stroke at a shallow angle, and lower it into the pot. Bake covered for twenty minutes, then remove the lid and bake another twenty to twenty-five minutes until the crust is deep brown. Let the loaf cool on a rack for at least an hour before cutting; the crumb is still setting, and a hot slice turns gummy. Keep the leftovers cut side down on a board, and toast the second day's slices with butter.

Common problems: a dense crumb usually means the starter was weak or the dough was shaped too early; a flat loaf means it proofed too long; a pale crust means the oven was cool or the pot was not preheated. Change one thing at a time and write the numbers down.
