Photosynthesis is the fundamental process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy, creating the organic compounds necessary for life. This process occurs primarily within the chloroplasts, specialized organelles found in plant cells that contain the pigment chlorophyll. Chlorophyll is responsible for absorbing light, particularly in the blue and red wavelengths, while reflecting green light, which gives plants their characteristic color. When light strikes the chloroplast, it excites electrons within the chlorophyll molecules, initiating a series of reactions known as the light-dependent reactions. These reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes, which are stacked structures inside the chloroplast that resemble flattened sacs.

During the light-dependent reactions, the energy from the absorbed photons is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons, and electrons. This splitting of water, known as photolysis, releases oxygen as a byproduct, which is then released into the atmosphere. The electrons generated from this process travel through an electron transport chain embedded in the thylakoid membrane. As these electrons move along the chain, they release energy that is used to pump protons across the membrane, creating a concentration gradient. This gradient drives the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, through a process called chemiosmosis. Additionally, the electrons are eventually transferred to a molecule called NADP+, reducing it to NADPH. Both ATP and NADPH serve as energy carriers that will be used in the next stage of photosynthesis.

The second phase of photosynthesis is the Calvin cycle, also known as the light-independent reactions, which occurs in the stroma, the fluid-filled space surrounding the thylakoids. Unlike the light-dependent reactions, the Calvin cycle does not directly require light, but it relies entirely on the ATP and NADPH produced in the previous stage. The cycle begins with carbon fixation, where a molecule of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is attached to a five-carbon sugar called ribulose bisphosphate. This reaction is catalyzed by an enzyme called RuBisCO. The resulting six-carbon compound is unstable and immediately splits into two three-carbon molecules called 3-phosphoglycerate.

These three-carbon molecules are then reduced using the energy from ATP and the electrons from NADPH. This reduction step converts the molecules into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, a simple sugar. While some of these sugar molecules are used to build larger carbohydrates like glucose, which the plant uses for energy and structural purposes, most are recycled to regenerate ribulose bisphosphate. This regeneration step requires additional ATP and ensures that the cycle can continue as long as carbon dioxide and light energy are available. Ultimately, the Calvin cycle transforms inorganic carbon dioxide into organic glucose, storing the energy captured from sunlight in a stable chemical form that sustains the plant and provides food for other organisms in the ecosystem.
