The First World War was not the result of a single event but rather the culmination of deep-seated tensions that had been building for decades, primarily driven by aggressive nationalism, imperial competition, and the militarization of European society. The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw a fierce scramble for colonies and influence, particularly between the major powers of Britain, France, Germany, and Russia. This competition created a volatile atmosphere where economic and military rivalry became central to foreign policy. Additionally, the rise of intense nationalism in the Balkans and within the Austro-Hungarian Empire, where diverse ethnic groups sought independence or greater autonomy, destabilized the region. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by a Serbian nationalist in June 1914 served as the immediate trigger, but it was the underlying structural weaknesses of the international order that made such a tragedy catastrophic.

The system of alliances played a the critical role in transforming this localized crisis into a continental war. Europe had divided into two opposing blocs: the Triple Entente, consisting of Britain, France, and Russia, and the Central Powers, led by Germany and Austria-Hungary, with Italy initially aligned with the latter before switching sides. These alliances were not merely diplomatic agreements but military commitments that bound the powers to support each other in the event of conflict. When Austria-Hungary issued an ultimatum to Serbia following the assassination, Serbia’s refusal led to Austria declaring war. Because Russia was a protector of Slavic interests and had a treaty obligation to support Serbia, it began to mobilize its troops. This Russian mobilization alarmed Germany, which viewed it as a direct threat to its security and activated its own alliance obligations to support Austria-Hungary.

As the domino effect of mobilizations continued, the rigid alliance structure left little room for diplomatic de-escalation. Germany’s decision to declare war on Russia and subsequently on France, based on the Schlieffen Plan to quickly defeat France before turning to Russia, escalated the conflict beyond the Balkans. Britain, bound by its alliance with France and concerns over German expansion into Belgium, eventually entered the war. Thus, what began as a regional dispute between Austria-Hungary and Serbia rapidly engulfed the major powers because the alliance system created a chain reaction of mutual obligations. Each nation felt compelled to act to protect its allies and secure its own strategic interests, turning a local assassination into a global catastrophe that drew in empires and colonies across the world.
