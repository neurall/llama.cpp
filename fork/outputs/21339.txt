The First World War was not the result of a single event but rather the culmination of deep-seated tensions that had been building for decades, primarily driven by aggressive nationalism, imperial competition, and the militarization of European society. The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw a fierce scramble for colonies and influence, particularly between the major powers of Britain, France, Germany, and Russia. This competition created a climate of mutual suspicion and fear, where each nation viewed the expansion of its rivals as a direct threat to its own security and prestige. Simultaneously, the rise of intense nationalism within multi-ethnic empires, such as the Austro-Hungarian Empire and the Ottoman Empire, destabilized the region, as ethnic groups sought independence or greater autonomy, often supported by neighboring powers. This internal fragmentation combined with external imperial ambitions to create a volatile powder keg in the Balkans, where the interests of the great powers frequently clashed.

The system of alliances, which was intended to maintain peace through deterrence, instead created a rigid framework that made diplomatic resolution nearly impossible. Europe was divided into two opposing blocs: the Triple Entente comprising Britain, France, and Russia, and the Central Powers led by Germany and Austria-Hungary, with Italy initially aligned with the latter before switching sides. These alliances were not merely defensive pacts but often included mutual obligations that required military support regardless of the specific circumstances of a conflict. When the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by a Serbian nationalist in June 1914 occurred, it was initially a regional crisis involving Austria-Hungary and Serbia. However, the alliance structures immediately activated a chain reaction. Austria-Hungary, backed by Germany, issued an ultimatum to Serbia, which was rejected. Serbia, in turn, relied on the support of Russia, which viewed itself as the protector of Slavic peoples and saw the crisis as an opportunity to expand its influence in the Balkans.

As diplomatic efforts failed, the rigid alliance commitments forced the great powers into a corner where backing down was seen as a sign of weakness. Germany, fearing encirclement by the Entente, gave Austria-Hungary a "blank check" to support its actions against Serbia. Russia, mobilizing its army to support Serbia, triggered a German response that viewed Russian mobilization as an act of war. Consequently, Germany declared war on Russia and France, while Britain, bound by its alliance with France and concerns over German expansion into Belgium, entered the conflict. Thus, a local dispute in Sarajevo escalated rapidly into a continental war because the alliance system removed the flexibility needed for de-escalation. Each nation felt compelled to honor its treaty obligations, turning a regional assassination into a global catastrophe where the interconnectedness of the alliances ensured that no major power could remain neutral once the first shots were fired.
