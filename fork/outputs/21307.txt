   *(Paragraph 1: Introduction & Chloroplast Structure)*
   Photosynthesis is the fundamental biological process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules. This process occurs within specialized organelles called chloroplasts, which contain a network of membrane-bound compartments known as thylakoids. The thylakoid membranes house the light-absorbing pigments, primarily chlorophyll, along with a series of protein complexes that work together to capture solar energy and transform it into usable chemical forms. The overall goal of photosynthesis is to produce glucose and other carbohydrates while releasing oxygen as a byproduct, but this transformation happens through two interconnected phases: the light-dependent reactions and the light-independent reactions, commonly referred to as the Calvin cycle.

   *(Paragraph 2: Light Absorption & Light-Dependent Reactions)*
   When sunlight reaches the chloroplast, photons are absorbed by chlorophyll molecules embedded in the thylakoid membranes. These pigments are organized into two major protein complexes called photosystem II and photosystem I. Upon absorbing light, electrons in chlorophyll are excited to a higher energy state and transferred to an electron acceptor, initiating a chain of electron movements. This electron flow travels through a series of carriers in the thylakoid membrane, creating an electron transport chain that pumps protons from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen. The accumulation of protons establishes an electrochemical gradient across the membrane, which drives the enzyme ATP synthase to convert adenosine diphosphate into adenosine triphosphate. Meanwhile, water molecules are split in a process called photolysis, releasing oxygen gas, protons, and electrons that replenish the electrons lost by chlorophyll. As electrons continue through the transport chain and eventually reach photosystem I, they are re-energized by additional light absorption and ultimately transferred to a carrier molecule called NADP+, forming NADPH. Together, ATP and NADPH serve as the chemical energy carriers that will power the next phase of photosynthesis.

   *(Paragraph 3: The Calvin Cycle)*
   The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma, the fluid-filled space surrounding the thylakoids, and does not directly require light, though it depends entirely on the ATP and NADPH produced by the light-dependent reactions. The cycle begins with carbon fixation, where a molecule of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is attached to a five-carbon sugar called ribulose bisphosphate by the enzyme RuBisCO. This reaction produces an unstable six-carbon intermediate that immediately splits into two molecules of three-carbon compounds called 3-phosphoglycerate. These molecules are then reduced using energy from ATP and electrons from NADPH, converting them into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, a three-carbon sugar that can be used to build glucose and other organic molecules. To keep the cycle running, most of the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate is recycled through a series of enzymatic steps that regenerate ribulose bisphosphate, requiring additional ATP. Only a small fraction of the produced sugars exits the cycle to be used by the plant for growth, storage, or metabolism. The Calvin cycle effectively transforms inorganic carbon into organic carbohydrates, completing the conversion of light energy into stable chemical bonds.
