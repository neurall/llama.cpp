The First World War emerged from a complex web of long-standing tensions rooted in imperialism, nationalism, and militarism. European powers had engaged in a fierce competition for colonies and economic dominance, creating rivalries that strained diplomatic relations and fostered mutual suspicion. The accumulation of these grievances created a volatile environment where the balance of power was fragile, and the mobilization of troops was often seen as a necessary deterrent rather than a mere defensive measure.

The immediate spark of the conflict originated in the Balkans, a region where the interests of the great powers clashed with local nationalist movements. Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1908 angered Serbia, which viewed the region as part of a future unified Slavic state and saw Austrian expansion as a threat to its own sovereignty. This rivalry intensified after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by a Serbian nationalist in June 1914. Austria-Hungary, seeking to crush Serbian influence and deter further agitation, issued an ultimatum that effectively demanded submission. Serbia's refusal to accept all terms led Austria-Hungary to declare war, transforming a diplomatic dispute into a localized military confrontation between two neighboring states. This regional clash was the catalyst that activated the dormant tensions across the continent.

The escalation from a regional war to a global conflict was driven by the rigid system of alliances that had divided Europe into opposing blocs. Austria-Hungary relied on the support of Germany, which provided a "blank check" of unconditional backing, encouraging Vienna to pursue a hard line against Serbia. Germany's involvement quickly drew in Russia, which viewed itself as the protector of Slavic nations and felt compelled to support Serbia to maintain its influence in the Balkans and counter Austrian expansion. As Russia mobilized its forces, Germany perceived this as a direct threat to its security and initiated its own mobilization, activating the military mechanisms of the Central Powers. The alliance structure meant that the conflict was no longer bilateral; the mobilization of one power necessitated the response of its allies, creating a domino effect that rapidly expanded the theater of war and locked the nations into a cycle of escalating military preparations.

The involvement of the United Kingdom further solidified the transformation of the war into a confrontation among the great powers. Britain was part of the Triple Entente with France and Russia, though its commitments were less formal than the military alliances of the Central Powers. Britain declared war on Germany, bringing its vast empire and naval resources into the conflict. This entry ensured that the war was no longer confined to continental Europe but became a global struggle involving the major economic and military powers of the era.
