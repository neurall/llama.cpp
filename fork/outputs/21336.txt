Photosynthesis is the fundamental process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy, creating the organic compounds necessary for life. This process occurs primarily within the chloroplasts, specialized organelles found in plant cells that contain the pigment chlorophyll. Chlorophyll is responsible for absorbing light, particularly in the blue and red wavelengths, while reflecting green light, which gives plants their characteristic color. When light strikes the chloroplast, it excites electrons within the chlorophyll molecules, initiating a series of reactions known as the light-dependent reactions. These reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes, which are stacked structures inside the chloroplast that resemble flattened sacs.

During the light-dependent reactions, the energy from the absorbed photons is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons, and electrons. This splitting of water, known as photolysis, releases oxygen as a byproduct, which is then released into the atmosphere. The electrons generated from this process travel through an electron transport chain embedded in the thylakoid membrane. As these electrons move along the chain, they release energy that is used to pump hydrogen ions into the thylakoid space, creating a concentration gradient. This gradient drives the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, through an enzyme called ATP synthase. Simultaneously, the electrons are used to reduce nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, or NADP+, into NADPH. Both ATP and NADPH serve as energy carriers that will be used in the next stage of photosynthesis.

The second stage of photosynthesis is the Calvin cycle, also known as the light-independent reactions, which occurs in the stroma, the fluid-filled space surrounding the thylakoids. Unlike the light-dependent reactions, the Calvin cycle does not directly require light, but it relies on the ATP and NADPH produced earlier. The cycle begins with carbon fixation, where a molecule of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is attached to a five-carbon sugar called ribulose bisphosphate. This reaction is catalyzed by an enzyme called RuBisCO. The resulting six-carbon compound is unstable and immediately splits into two three-carbon molecules called 3-phosphoglycerate.

These three-carbon molecules are then reduced using the energy from ATP and the electrons from NADPH. This reduction step converts the molecules into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, a simple sugar. While some of these sugar molecules are used to build larger carbohydrates like glucose, which the plant uses for energy and structural purposes, most are recycled to regenerate ribulose bisphosphate. This regeneration allows the cycle to continue, ensuring a constant supply of carbon dioxide fixation. Ultimately, the Calvin cycle produces glucose and other organic compounds that serve as the building blocks for the plant’s growth and metabolism, while the oxygen released during the light-dependent reactions supports aerobic life on Earth.
